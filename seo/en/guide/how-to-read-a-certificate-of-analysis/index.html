How to read a certificate of analysis

A certificate of analysis (COA) shows in black and white what’s inside a CBD product. We explain step by step what to look for — with a checklist to tick off.

What is a certificate of analysis?

A COA is a lab’s test report for a specific production batch. It documents which cannabinoids are present and in what amounts, and whether the product is free of unwanted substances. Reputable sellers provide the certificate for every batch — you’ll find our key values on the lab reports page.

At first glance, lab reports look complicated: lots of abbreviations, tables and units. With the following five steps you can read any certificate in a few minutes.

Step 1: Check the lab, date and accreditation

At the top of the certificate you’ll find the lab’s name and address, the test date and usually a report number. Make sure it is an independent lab — not the manufacturer’s own. Accreditation to ISO/IEC 17025 is a good sign: this standard sets out the requirements testing and calibration laboratories must meet regarding their competence and the reliability of their results.

The date matters too: a report that is several years old says little about the goods on the shelf today.

Step 2: Match the batch number

Each certificate applies to one specific batch (or lot) only. Compare the batch number on the certificate with the number on your product. If they don’t match, the report belongs to a different production run — and tells you nothing about your product.

Step 3: Read the cannabinoid profile

In the main section, the lab lists the cannabinoids measured, typically CBD, CBDA, CBG, CBN, Δ9-THC and THCA. Values are given in percent (%), milligrams per gram (mg/g) or milligrams per millilitre (mg/ml). As a guide: 1% equals 10 mg/g.

  • CBD: compare the value with the label. Small deviations are normal, large ones are not.
  • CBDA: the acid form of CBD found in the raw plant. Heating (decarboxylation) turns it into CBD.
  • CBG, CBN & co.: other cannabinoids in small amounts — typical of full-spectrum products.
  • ND or < LOQ: “not detected” or “below the limit of quantification” — the substance was absent or present only in non-measurable amounts.

Step 4: Assess the THC content correctly

In Austria, the THC limit of 0.3% is decisive. Don’t just look at Δ9-THC but also at THCA. THCA itself is not intoxicating, but it converts to THC when heated. Many labs therefore state a total THC value, calculated as follows:

Total THC = Δ9-THC + 0.877 × THCA. The factor 0.877 accounts for the part of the molecule lost as carbon dioxide during conversion.

An example: a sample with 0.10% Δ9-THC and 0.20% THCA has a total THC content of 0.10 + 0.877 × 0.20 ≈ 0.28% — just below the limit.

Step 5: Contaminants and residues

A complete certificate tests not only cannabinoids but also possible contaminants. You’ll often find these sections on separate pages or in separate tables:

  • Pesticides: residues of plant protection products.
  • Heavy metals: such as lead, cadmium, mercury and arsenic.
  • Solvents: residues from extraction, for example ethanol or butane.
  • Microbiology: moulds, yeasts and bacteria.
  • Mycotoxins: toxins produced by moulds, such as aflatoxins.

Each line shows the measured value, the permitted limit and a result such as “pass”. What matters is that every test has been passed. If these sections are missing entirely, the product was probably only tested for cannabinoids.

Checklist: check a COA in two minutes

  1. Is the report from an independent, accredited lab?
  2. Is the test date recent?
  3. Does the batch number match your product?
  4. Does the CBD value match the label?
  5. Is total THC below 0.3%?
  6. Were pesticides, heavy metals and solvents tested — and passed?

Frequently asked questions

What does COA stand for?

COA stands for certificate of analysis. It is a lab’s test report for a specific batch of a product.

What does “ND” mean on a lab report?

ND stands for “not detected” — the substance was not found or was below the lab’s detection limit.

Why does the CBD value differ slightly from the label?

Hemp is a natural product and every measuring method has tolerances. Deviations of a few tenths of a percent are normal.

How often should a product be tested?

Ideally every batch. Only then does the certificate really match the product you buy.
